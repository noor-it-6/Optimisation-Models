Aggregate planning models · SureStep

00

Problem type: aggregate planning models

An aggregate planning model plans production, workforce and inventory over several periods so that forecast demand is met at least total cost. It is a multi-period model: what you decide this month changes what is possible next month.

How to recognise one

  • Time is split into periods (usually months) with a forecast demand in each.
  • Periods are linked by balance equations: stock and workforce carry over from one period to the next.
  • Capacity depends on the workforce, which can be changed by hiring and firing at a cost.
  • There are several ways to meet a peak: build stock early, work overtime, hire, or (if allowed) deliver late.

Balance equations link the periods

Every multi-period model has the same pattern: what you end with equals what you started with, plus what came in, minus what went out.

\[\text{end}_t = \text{end}_{t-1} + \text{in}_t - \text{out}_t\]

Inventory: in = production, out = demand. Workforce: in = hires, out = fires.

Three classic strategies

StrategyHow it meets a peakMain cost
ChaseHire for the peak, fire after itHiring and firing
LevelSteady workforce, build stock before the peakHolding inventory
MixedSome stock, some overtime, some workforce changeA balance of all

The optimisation model does not pick a strategy in advance. It finds the cheapest mix, which is usually "mixed".

Decisions vs consequencesThe hardest step is deciding which quantities you choose (hires, fires, overtime, production) and which follow from them (workforce, inventory, cost).
End effectsThe model ignores everything after the last period, so it may run stock to zero or keep or cut staff in ways that look odd. A rolling horizon reduces this.
Whole workersHiring and firing must be whole numbers, so the model is an integer program. The LP answer is close but not usable as it stands.
Avoid IF and MAXHolding and shortage costs written with IF make the model nonsmooth; a linear reformulation solves reliably.

Check yourself: is it an aggregate planning model?

A hospital plans monthly nurse hiring and agency hours against forecast patient days for the next year.

Yes, a service version. There is no inventory (care cannot be stored), but the workforce balance, hiring and firing costs and overtime (agency) are the same.

An ice cream maker builds stock in spring to meet its summer peak, with a fixed factory workforce.

Yes. It is aggregate planning with the workforce fixed: a level strategy, trading holding cost against capacity.

Briggs chooses how many staff start their five-day week on each day.

No. That repeats the same week; nothing carries over between periods. It is an employee scheduling model.

01

Planning four months of shoe production at SureStep

SureStep must meet demand for shoes on time over the next four months. It starts with 500 pairs in stock and 100 workers. It can hire or fire at the start of each month and use some overtime.

Regular wage$1,500 per worker per month
Regular hours160 per worker per month
Overtimeup to 20 h per worker, $13 per h
Hire / fire$1,600 / $2,000 per worker
Per pair4 labour hours, $15 raw material
Holding cost$3 per pair left at month end

What does one pair cost in labour?

Regular time: $1,500 ÷ 160 h = $9.375 per hour, × 4 h = $37.50 a pair. Overtime: $13 × 4 h = $52 a pair, so overtime is the dearest way to make shoes. But wages are paid per worker whether or not they are busy, so using spare regular hours adds only the $15 of material.

Predict first: can 100 workers meet month 2?

No. 100 workers × 160 h ÷ 4 h = 4,000 pairs, or 4,500 with full overtime, but month 2 needs 5,000. SureStep must build stock in month 1, hire, or both.

Decisions and their consequences

Two chains run through the months

The workforce chain: workers last month + hired − fired = workers this month, which sets regular hours and the overtime limit. The inventory chain: stock last month + produced − demand = stock this month. Month 2 cannot be planned without months 1 and 3.

Variables in the maths, formulas in Excel

In the formulation, workforce \(W_t\) and inventory \(I_t\) are variables tied down by equality constraints. In Excel they are formulas, so Solver only changes hires, fires, overtime and production. Both describe the same model.

The objective (1) minimises total cost over the four months: hiring, firing, regular wages, overtime, raw materials and holding inventory. Constraints (2) are the workforce balance: workers this month equal workers last month plus hires minus fires. Constraints (3) ensure that the labour hours needed for production do not exceed regular plus overtime hours available. Constraints (4) limit overtime to 20 hours per worker. Constraints (5) are the inventory balance: stock at the end of the month equals stock at the start plus production minus demand. Constraints (6) keep every quantity non-negative; in particular \(I_t \ge 0\) means demand must be met on time, with no stockouts. Constraints (7) require whole numbers of workers to be hired and fired.

Where is \(I_t \ge 0\)?

"Inventory after production ≥ forecasted demand" is \(I_{t-1} + P_t \ge D_t\), which rearranges to \(I_t \ge 0\). It is the no-stockout part of constraint (6).

06

Allowing backlogging: three versions

Suppose customers will accept late delivery at a shortage cost of $20 per pair per month. Inventory can now go negative. There are two ways to model the costs, and only one is safe.

Total cost690,180$
Saving vs no backlog2,640$
Why can't both \(I_t^+\) and \(I_t^-\) be positive?

Nothing in the constraints stops it. But if both were positive, reducing each by the same amount keeps \(I_t^+ - I_t^-\) unchanged and lowers cost, because both \(C^I\) and \(C^S\) are positive. So at the optimum at least one is zero, and the linear model behaves exactly like the IF version.

When is late delivery worth it?

Change the shortage cost per pair per month in the linear backlog model (Model 3). The model is re-solved as you drag.

$0$10$20$30

Pairs backlogged520pair-months over the 4 months
Why does backlogging stop at $26?

A late pair saves SureStep the cost of making it earlier and holding it, or of keeping workers it would later fire. Once the shortage penalty is higher than those savings, meeting demand on time is cheaper, and Model 3 gives the same plan as Model 1 ($692,820).

At $0, why is any demand met on time at all?

Backlog must be cleared by the end of month 4, and the workforce still has to make every pair. Free lateness lets SureStep smooth production, but it cannot skip it.

08

Using the model month by month

In practice the plan is not followed for all four months. SureStep re-plans every month with fresh forecasts: a rolling planning horizon.

  1. Treat the demands as forecasts and solve the four-month model.
  2. Implement only month 1's production and hiring and firing decisions.
  3. At the end of month 1, observe actual demand, then update the forecasts for the next four months.
  4. Re-solve, starting from the real inventory and workforce, and implement the new month 2 decisions. Repeat.

Why roll the horizon?

Forecasts further ahead are less accurate, and a fixed four-month model has end effects: it does not care what happens in month 5. Re-solving every month keeps the plan based on the newest information and always looks four months ahead.
