Problem type: nonlinear pricing models

In a nonlinear programming (NLP) model the objective or constraints are not straight-line functions of the decisions. Pricing is the classic case: revenue is price × quantity, and quantity itself depends on price, so revenue curves. Solver uses its GRG Nonlinear method, which climbs uphill from a starting point and stops at the top of the nearest hill.

How to recognise one

  • A decision multiplies another decision, as in price × demand when demand depends on price.
  • There is a demand function linking quantity to price, often with cross-effects between products or periods.
  • Risk is measured by a variance (squares and products of weights), as in portfolio models.
  • Costs or returns have economies or diseconomies of scale.

Local and global optima

A local optimum beats every nearby point; the global optimum beats every point. GRG can stop at a local optimum. If the profit surface has a single peak (it is concave), the local optimum is global. If you are not sure, run Solver from several starting points, or use Solver's Multistart option.

Check yourself: is it nonlinear?

A cinema sets one ticket price; attendance falls by 20 people for every £1 increase.

Yes. Revenue = price × (a − 20 × price), which has a price-squared term.

Great Threads chooses how many shorts and jackets to make, each with a fixed price.

Not nonlinear in the same way. Revenue is price × quantity with fixed prices, which is linear; the fixed costs are handled with binary variables (14.9).

An investor chooses weights to minimise the variance of portfolio return.

Yes. Variance contains squared weights and products of weights: a quadratic model (14.12).

Setting peak and off-peak prices at Florida Power and Light

FPL must set a price per mWh for on-peak and off-peak periods, and decide how much generating capacity to keep. Demand in each period depends on both prices. Capacity costs $75 per mWh a month and must cover demand in both periods. Which prices and capacity maximise monthly profit?

Monthly demand (millions of mWh), prices in $ per mWh:

\[D_{peak} = 2.253 - 0.013P_{peak} + 0.003P_{off}\]
\[D_{off} = 1.142 + 0.005P_{peak} - 0.015P_{off}\]

A higher peak price cuts peak demand and pushes some use into the off-peak period, and the other way round.

Predict first: why not charge the same price all day?

Capacity has to be built for the busier period. A higher peak price shifts some demand off-peak, so FPL needs less capacity for the same total use. Try it in stage 02.

Model formulation

The model formulation uses the notation in the table. The objective multiplies prices by demands that depend on prices, which makes it nonlinear.

Objective function:

\[\max \sum_{t\in T} P_t \Big(A_t + \sum_{s\in T} B_{ts} P_s\Big) - C K\]
(1)
Example

Subject to:

\[A_t + \sum_{s\in T} B_{ts} P_s \;\le\; K \qquad \forall t \in T\]
(2)
Example
\[P_t \ge 0 \quad \forall t \in T; \qquad K \ge 0\]
(3)
Example
Table 1. Model notation table.
NotationDescription
Sets\(T\)Set of periods, indexed by \(t\) and \(s\), \(T = \{peak, off\}\)
Parameters\(A_t\)Constant term of demand in period \(t \in T\) (millions of mWh)
\(B_{ts}\)Change in demand in period \(t\) per $1 rise in the price in period \(s\)
\(C\)Monthly cost of capacity, $75 per mWh
Decision variables\(P_t\)Price per mWh in period \(t \in T\) ($)
\(K\)Capacity kept (millions of mWh a month)
Variables32 prices + capacity
Constraints2demand ≤ capacity
Model typeNLPquadratic objective

The objective (1) maximises monthly profit: revenue in each period, the price times the demand that price produces, minus the cost of the capacity kept. Constraints (2) ensure that capacity is enough to meet demand in both periods. Constraints (3) keep prices and capacity non-negative. The demand expression inside (1) and (2) is the same: each period's demand falls with its own price and rises with the other period's price.

Why SUMPRODUCT of Demands and Prices is nonlinear

Both Demands and Prices change as Solver works, so their product contains price-squared terms. That is what makes Simplex LP unusable here.

Why both capacity constraints bind

If peak demand were higher than off-peak, FPL could raise the peak price a little: peak revenue per mWh rises, peak demand falls, and less capacity is needed. That keeps paying until the two demands meet. The best prices lie on the dashed line.

Why does each extra $1 of capacity cost raise both prices by about 25 cents?

Dearer capacity makes it worth keeping less of it, so FPL raises both prices to reduce demand. In the SolverTable sheet each $2 rise in capacity cost raises both prices by $0.50 and reduces capacity by 0.005 million mWh.
