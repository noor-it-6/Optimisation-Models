Asset–liability matching · Armco

00

Problem type: asset–liability matching

An asset–liability matching model buys assets today, usually bonds, whose future cash flows will meet a schedule of payments that must be made later. It finds the cheapest set of assets that pays every bill on time, with any surplus reinvested at a safe rate.

How to recognise one

  • There is a fixed schedule of future payments (liabilities) that cannot be missed.
  • Assets bought now pay known amounts at known times: coupons, then a maturity payment.
  • Cash left over in a year is carried forward with interest; nothing can be borrowed.
  • The aim is the least money set aside today.

The key modelling idea: count the money set aside, not just the bonds

The first payment is due before any bond pays out, so the fund must keep some cash back. The model needs a variable for the total cash allocated today: part buys bonds and the rest is held as cash for year 1.

Check yourself: is it an asset–liability matching model?

A family buys savings bonds now to pay school fees each September for the next ten years.

Yes. Fixed future payments are met from bonds bought today, with spare cash carried forward.

Barney-Jones chooses investments to have as much cash as possible after four years.

No. There are no fixed payments to meet; it maximises final wealth. It is the investment strategy model (14.6).

A council sets aside money today so that a bridge repair fund can pay contractors £2m in each of the next five years.

Yes. It is the same structure as Armco: known outgoings, assets with known cash flows, and the least money set aside.

Model typeLPcash-flow matching

The objective (1) minimises the total cash Armco must allocate today. Constraint (2) is the year 1 balance: the cash allocated pays for the bonds, makes the first payment, and anything left over is carried forward. Constraints (3) are the balances for years 2 to 15: cash carried from last year with interest, plus the coupons and maturity payments received, pays this year's pension, and the remainder is carried forward. Constraints (4) ensure that nothing is negative; in particular \(E_t \ge 0\) means every payment is made in full and on time.

Where is \(E_t\)?

Excel never names it. \(E_t\) is Cash_available minus Cash_required in each column, which is why the next year's formula starts with (B20-B22). The constraint Cash_available ≥ Cash_required is exactly \(E_t \ge 0\).

What if bonds must be bought whole?

The LP buys fractions of a bond. Adding integer constraints, as in the "Model with Integers" sheet, costs a little more.

Why not just round the LP answer?

Rounding 73.69, 77.21 and 28.84 to the nearest whole bonds gives 74, 77 and 29. With those bonds Armco needs $197,974.68 to meet every payment, $87 more than the integer optimum of 74, 79 and 27 ($197,887.28). Rounding gives a workable plan, but not the cheapest one.

Why does Bond 3 stay at 28.84 for every rate?

Year 15's payment of $31,000 can only come from Bond 3's maturity payment of $1,075 (plus any cash carried in). The cheapest way is to carry nothing into year 15, so 31,000 ÷ 1,075 = 28.84 bonds, whatever the rate.
