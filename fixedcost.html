Problem type: fixed-cost (fixed-charge) models

A fixed-cost model has an activity that costs a lump sum as soon as you do any of it, whatever the amount: renting a machine, opening a line, running a delivery route. That jump makes the cost nonlinear. A binary variable for "do it or not", linked to the amount by a big-M constraint, turns the model back into a linear integer model that Solver can solve reliably.

How to recognise one

  • An activity has a cost per unit plus a one-off cost if it happens at all.
  • The one-off cost is zero if the activity is not used.
  • You must choose which activities to run and how much of each.
  • Shared resources (labour, material) limit the amounts.

The key modelling idea: link the amount to a 0/1 switch

For each product, a binary \(Y_p\) says whether its machine is rented. The constraint \(X_p \le M_p Y_p\) says: if \(Y_p = 0\), nothing can be made; if \(Y_p = 1\), up to \(M_p\) can be made. The fixed cost is then simply \(F_p Y_p\).

Check yourself: is it a fixed-cost model?

A bakery pays £400 a day to run its croissant line, plus 20p per croissant, and decides how many to bake.

Yes. The £400 is incurred only if any croissants are baked: a fixed charge with a variable amount.

Tatham picks which of seven projects to fund within a budget.

Not quite. There is a binary choice, but no variable amount to link to it: each project is simply in or out. It is a capital budgeting (knapsack) model.

A retailer opens warehouses, each with an annual cost, and decides how much to ship through each.

Yes. Opening cost is fixed; shipments are variable and only possible through open warehouses: \(X \le M Y\) again.

Predict first: jackets earn the most per unit. Should Great Threads make only jackets?

Jackets earn $75 each but need 8 labour hours, so labour allows only 500 a week: $37,500 less $1,600 rent. Shorts earn $30 per labour hour against jackets' $9.38. Stage 05 shows the best mix uses both.

02

Why a fixed cost breaks an ordinary LP

Profit from one product, as a function of how many are made. Choose a product.

A jump at zero

Making nothing earns $0. Making one unit earns the margin minus the whole rental. The line jumps at zero, so profit is not proportional to the amount made, and an LP cannot represent it.

What happens with IF functions

The workbook's "Using IF Function" sheet sets Rent = IF(Units > 0, 1, 0). GRG Nonlinear then stopped at a plan making 1,000 skirts for $36,900, far below the true optimum of $54,614. With jumps in the model, Solver cannot see which direction improves profit.

Model typeMIPmixed integer

The objective (1) maximises weekly profit: the contribution of each unit made, minus the rental for every product whose machinery is rented. Constraints (2) ensure that labour hours and cloth used do not exceed what is available. Constraints (3) link production to rental: a product can only be made if its machinery is rented, and then no more than \(M_p\) units. Constraints (4) keep amounts non-negative and make each rental decision a yes or no.

06

How big should M be?

Replace each product's \(M_p\) by one common value and re-solve.

1009665,00010,000
Best profit54,614$ a week
LP relaxation bound57,060$ if binaries could be fractions

Too small cuts off good plans

M is a cap on production. Below 965.5 it stops Great Threads making the 965.5 pairs of shorts the best plan needs, so profit falls. That is a modelling error, not a real limit.

Too big makes Solver work harder

Any M above 965.5 gives the right answer, but a larger M lets the LP relaxation pretend a machine is "slightly rented" (for example \(Y = 0.05\)) and pay only 5% of its rent. The bound gets weaker and branch and bound has more to search. The workbook's choice, the most each product could ever use, is as tight as possible.

What if prices or rules change?

The skirt price slider reproduces the SolverTable sheet. The rules reproduce and extend the "Extra Constraint" sheet.

$70$85$100
Profit54,614$ a week
Why does the plan switch completely at about $87?

Skirts alone use all the cloth at 1,000 units: profit 1,000 × (price − 30) − 1,500. That beats $54,614 once the price passes $86.11. Because rentals are all or nothing, the best plan jumps from shorts and jackets to skirts only, with nothing in between.

With "at least three machines", why is the third one idle?

The rule only says three machines must be rented, not used. The cheapest way to obey it is to rent a $1,500 machine and make nothing with it. To force real production, add a minimum amount for each product rented, as in the second rule.
