00

Problem type: covering and facility location models

A covering model decides where to put facilities (hubs, stations, clinics) so that people or places are "covered": close enough to be served. Each candidate site covers a known set of demand points. The model either covers everyone with as few sites as possible, or covers as much demand as possible with the sites it can afford.

How to recognise one

  • There are candidate sites and demand points (cities, zones, neighbourhoods).
  • A site covers a demand point if it is within a service standard: a distance, a travel time, a response time.
  • Each site is opened or not: a binary decision.
  • The aim is either fewest sites to cover everyone, or most demand covered with a limited number of sites or budget.

The key modelling idea: the coverage matrix

Everything about geography is boiled down to a 0/1 table \(A_{ij}\): 1 if site \(i\) covers demand point \(j\). Distances, travel times or business rules only matter through this table.

Check yourself: which covering model?

An ambulance service wants every village within 15 minutes of a station, using as few stations as possible.

Set covering: everyone must be covered and the number of stations is minimised.

A council can afford three new libraries and wants as many residents as possible within a mile of one.

Maximal covering: the number of sites is fixed and the population covered is maximised. Some residents may be left uncovered.

A retailer wants to minimise the total distance customers travel to its nearest store.

Not a covering model. Distance is measured, not just "within range or not": that is a p-median model.

Table 1. Model notation table.
NotationDescription
Sets\(I\)Set of cities, each a potential hub and a city to be served, indexed by \(i\) (hub) and \(j\) (city served)
Parameters\(D_{ij}\)Distance between cities \(i\) and \(j\) (miles)

Model typeIPset covering

The objective (1) minimises the number of hubs. Constraints (2) ensure that every city is within the service radius of at least one hub: for city \(j\), the sum counts the open hubs that cover it, and that count must be at least 1. Constraints (3) make each hub decision a yes or no.

An IF that is safe to use

The coverage table uses IF, yet the model is still linear. The IF only looks at distances, which are data; it never involves a changing cell. IF is only a problem when it depends on the decisions, as in the fixed-cost model (14.9).

When you can't cover everyone: maximal covering

Suppose Western can only afford a limited number of hubs. Now it cannot cover every city, so it should cover as many passengers as possible. This needs a new variable for each city: is it covered or not?

Objective function:

\[\max \sum_{j\in I} W_j Z_j\]
(1′)
Example

Subject to:

\[Z_j \;\le\; \sum_{i\in I} A_{ij} X_i \qquad \forall j \in I\]
(2′)
Example
\[\sum_{i\in I} X_i \;\le\; p\]
(3′)
Example
\[X_i \in \{0,1\},\;\; Z_j \in \{0,1\} \qquad \forall i, j \in I\]
(4′)

New notation: \(W_j\) = passengers at city \(j\) (thousands a day); \(p\) = most hubs allowed; \(Z_j\) = 1 if city \(j\) is covered, 0 otherwise.

The objective (1′) maximises the passengers in covered cities. Constraints (2′) are the key new idea: city \(j\) can only count as covered if at least one open hub covers it. If no open hub covers \(j\), the right side is 0, so \(Z_j\) must be 0. Because the objective rewards \(Z_j = 1\), Solver sets it to 1 whenever it is allowed. Constraint (3′) limits the number of hubs; with different opening costs it would be a budget, \(\sum_i F_i X_i \le B\). Constraints (4′) make both sets of decisions yes or no.

Why does weighting change the answer with two hubs?

Counting cities, Atlanta and Salt Lake City cover 11 of 12, missing only Boston. Counting passengers, New York (or Pittsburgh) and Salt Lake City do better: they reach Boston's 40 thousand passengers and miss Houston and New Orleans, which together have only 35 thousand. The objective decides which cities matter most.

Why is the constraint \(Z_j \le\) and not \(Z_j =\)?

The right side counts the open hubs covering \(j\), which can be 2 or 3. \(Z_j\) is a yes or no, so it cannot equal 2. "At most" says: covered only if at least one hub reaches it.

Why does coverage jump rather than grow smoothly as \(p\) increases?

Hubs are whole: each extra hub adds a block of cities at once. There is no "half a hub" to add half a city's passengers, which is also why Solver gives no shadow prices or Sensitivity Report for this model.
