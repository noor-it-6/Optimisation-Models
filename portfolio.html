Problem type: mean–variance portfolio models

A portfolio model decides what share of money to put into each investment. Investors want a high expected return and low risk, measured by the variance of return. Because variance involves products of the shares, the model is nonlinear: a quadratic program. The usual approach is to require a minimum expected return and minimise the variance.

How to recognise one

  • Money is split across investments whose returns are uncertain.
  • You know each investment's mean and standard deviation of return, and how they move together (correlations).
  • There are two goals, high return and low risk, handled by turning one into a constraint.
  • The decisions are weights: fractions of the money, adding up to 1.

The two formulas behind every portfolio model

\[\text{mean} = \sum_i W_i \mu_i \qquad \text{variance} = \sum_i \sum_j W_i W_j \sigma_{ij}, \quad \sigma_{ij} = \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j\]

The covariance \(\sigma_{ij}\) is large when two stocks are risky and tend to move together. Mixing stocks that do not move together cuts risk: diversification.

Check yourself: is it a portfolio model?

A pension fund splits money between equities, bonds and property to keep return volatility low while earning at least 5% a year on average.

Yes. Weights over assets, a minimum expected return, and risk measured by variance.

Barney-Jones times its investments over four years to have the most cash at the end.

No. Returns are known, not uncertain, and there is no variance: it is a linear multi-period model (14.6).

Tatham chooses whole projects to maximise NPV within a budget.

No. Decisions are yes or no and there is no risk term: a binary knapsack model (14.8).

The model formulation uses the notation in the table. The objective is quadratic; the constraints are linear.

Objective function:

\[\min \sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in I} W_i\, \sigma_{ij}\, W_j\]

Sets\(I\)Set of stocks, indexed by \(i\) and \(j\), \(I = \{1,2,3\}\)
Parameters\(\mu_i\)Mean annual return of stock \(i \in I\)

The objective (1) minimises the variance of the portfolio's return, the sum over every pair of stocks of their weights times their covariance. Constraint (2) ensures that all the money is invested. Constraint (3) ensures that the expected return of the portfolio is at least 12%. Constraints (4) rule out negative weights, which would mean short selling.

Reading the MMULT formula

MMULT(covariances, TRANSPOSE(weights)) gives a column: for each stock \(i\), \(\sum_j \sigma_{ij} W_j\). Multiplying by the weights row adds up \(W_i\) times each entry: exactly the double sum in (1). It is an array formula, entered with Ctrl+Shift+Enter in older versions of Excel.

Why does risk rise faster as the target goes up?

To earn more, the portfolio must hold more of stock 1, the riskiest. Each step moves money out of low-risk stock 3 into stock 1, and because variance grows with the square of the weight, risk climbs ever more steeply. The frontier curves upwards.

07

What if short selling is allowed?

Short selling means borrowing a stock and selling it, so its weight is negative. In the model, delete constraints (4).

Why sell stock 2 short?

Stock 2 moves closely with stock 3 (correlation 0.7). Holding stock 3 and selling some stock 2 short cancels part of their shared ups and downs, a hedge. The proceeds buy more stock 1 and 3, so risk falls a little further for the same 12% return.
