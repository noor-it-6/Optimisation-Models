Problem type: blending models

A blending model mixes several inputs to make one or more outputs. Each output must meet quality standards, and the quality of a blend is a weighted average of the qualities of what goes into it. The model chooses how much of each input goes into each output to maximise profit or minimise cost.

How to recognise one

  • Several inputs with known properties (quality, octane, protein, fat, sulphur) are mixed.
  • The property of a mix is the weighted average of the inputs' properties, weighted by quantity.
  • Each output has a specification: a minimum or maximum level of a property.
  • Inputs are limited or have a cost; outputs have a selling price.
  • The decisions are how much of input i goes into output j: one variable for every input and output pair.

The key modelling idea: clear the denominator

"Average quality ≥ 8" is a ratio, which Solver treats as nonlinear. Multiplying both sides by the total amount blended turns it into a linear constraint:

\[\frac{\sum_i Q_i X_{ij}}{\sum_i X_{ij}} \ge L_j \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; \sum_i Q_i X_{ij} \ge L_j \sum_i X_{ij}\]

This is valid because the amount blended is never negative, so the inequality does not flip.

Check yourself: is it a blending model?

A feed mill mixes maize, barley and soya into cattle feed with at least 16% protein at least cost.

Yes. Protein content of the mix is a weighted average of the ingredients' protein, and the specification is a minimum.

Grand Prix ships cars from three plants to four regions at least cost.

No. Nothing is mixed and there are no average-quality constraints. It is a transportation model.

A fund invests £10m across bonds so the average credit rating score is at least 7 and expected return is highest.

Yes, structurally. Bonds are the inputs, the portfolio is the blend, and "average rating ≥ 7" is a weighted-average constraint that linearises the same way.

Predict first: will Chandler sell any crude unblended?

Crude 1 sells for $65 and heating oil only $60, so using crude 1 in heating oil looks like a loss. But crude 2 is worth only $50 as crude and $60 as heating oil, and it needs some crude 1 to lift the blend to quality 6. Stage 05 shows that every barrel is blended.

Where 60% and 20% come from

With a share \(p\) of crude 1, quality is \(10p + 5(1-p) = 5 + 5p\). Gasoline needs \(5 + 5p \ge 8\), so \(p \ge 0.6\). Heating oil needs \(5 + 5p \ge 6\), so \(p \ge 0.2\). The constraint \(2X_{1G} - 3X_{2G} \ge 0\) in stage 03 says exactly the same thing as \(p \ge 0.6\).

With 5,000 bbl of crude 1, what is the most gasoline Chandler could make?

Gasoline must be at least 60% crude 1, so 5,000 bbl of crude 1 supports at most 5,000 ÷ 0.6 = 8,333 bbl of gasoline. That is exactly the plan Solver picks when gasoline sells for $80 or more (stage 06).

Model typeLPblending

The objective (1) maximises total revenue: the first term is revenue from selling the blended products and the second is revenue from selling unused crude oil. Constraints (2) ensure that for each crude oil, the total blended into all products does not exceed the barrels available. Constraints (3) ensure that for each product, the average quality of the blend meets the minimum level; they are the average-quality requirement with the denominator cleared, which keeps the model linear. Constraints (4) ensure that all blending amounts are non-negative.

Why not constrain E19:F19 directly?

E19 divides by Barrels_sold, a changing quantity, so the constraint would be nonlinear. Solver would need the slower GRG Nonlinear method and would fail with a divide-by-zero error if a product were not made at all.

05

The optimal plan earns $975,000

Every barrel of both crudes is blended. Both products meet their quality limit exactly.

Why does crude 1 go into heating oil at a "loss"?

Each barrel of crude 1 in heating oil earns $60 but gives up $65 of crude sales: −$5. Yet it lets 4 barrels of crude 2 into heating oil (the 20% rule), each earning $60 in place of $50: +$40. The pair is worth it.

Why does nothing change between $65 and $75?

In that range the same recipe stays optimal: the plan is fixed and only the revenue from it rises. The corners of the feasible region do not move when a price changes, so Solver stays at the same corner until another becomes better.

Why is a barrel of crude 1 worth $90 when it sells for only $65?

An extra barrel of crude 1 lets Chandler upgrade crude 2 from heating oil into gasoline while still meeting both quality limits. The shadow price counts all those knock-on gains. Above 15,000 bbl there is no crude 2 left to upgrade, and the value falls to $75, the price of gasoline.
