Problem type: logistics models

A company makes a product at some locations (origins) and must get it to other locations (destinations), sometimes through intermediate points. A logistics model decides how much to send along each route so that every destination gets what it needs, no origin ships more than it can, and total shipping cost is as low as possible.

How to recognise one

  • Something flows from where it is made or stored to where it is needed.
  • Each origin has a limited supply; each destination has a required amount.
  • Each route has a cost per unit shipped, and possibly a capacity.
  • The decisions are quantities on routes: one decision variable per arc.

Every node is one of three kinds

NodeMeaningFlow balance constraint
S OriginStarts with supply: positive net outflowNet outflow ≤ capacity (or = capacity)
T TransshipmentProduct only passes throughNet inflow = 0
D DestinationMust end up with demand: positive net inflowNet inflow ≥ demand (or = demand)

Net inflow = total inflow − total outflow. Net outflow is its negative. Arc capacities, where they exist, are simple upper bounds on each flow.

The family of models

Common extensionWhat changes in the model

Shrinkage or lossOnly a share α of inflow leaves a node (stage 07)

Quantity discountsCost per unit falls with volume: the model becomes nonlinear
Integer flowsIf all supplies, demands and arc capacities are whole numbers, the basic model always has an optimal solution with whole-number flows, so no integer constraints are needed. Shrinkage breaks this guarantee.
Fast to solveExcel Solver uses the simplex method. Specialist software uses the network simplex method, which is much faster and handles very large networks.
Feasibility checkTotal supply must be at least total demand, otherwise no plan can meet every destination. Check this before running Solver.
Where it is usedFood and grocery distribution, fuel and chemicals, retail supply chains, and moving stock between distribution centres and hospitals.

Check yourself: is it a logistics model?

A firm decides how many staff start their five-day week on each day so every day is covered.

No. Nothing flows between locations. This is a scheduling (covering) model: each variable counts staff, and each constraint makes sure a day has enough of them.

A food bank network moves donations from farms, through two depots, to local charities at least cost.

Yes. Farms are origins, depots are transshipment points and charities are destinations. It is a general logistics model like RedBrand.

A refinery mixes two crude oils into gasoline and heating oil that must meet quality levels.

No, although barrels "flow" into products. The defining constraints are quality averages, which makes it a blending model.

Getting tomato product to customers at least cost

Three kinds of node

Plants (S, nodes 1 to 3) start with supply. Warehouses (T, nodes 4 and 5) are transshipment points: product passes through and nothing is kept there. Customers (D, nodes 6 and 7) must end up with their demand.

Before you go on: how much spare capacity is there?

Plants can make 600 t and customers need 580 t, so only 20 t is spare. Keep this number in mind for stage 07.

Plants can ship to each other (for example 3 to 1 costs only 0.4), warehouses can ship to each other, and customer 6 can pass product on to customer 7 for 1. Any node can be a stepping stone, which is why constraints (2) to (4) in stage 03 use net flow.

The objective (1) minimises the total cost of shipping product across the network. Constraints (2) ensure that for each plant, the total shipped out minus the total received from other plants does not exceed the plant's production capacity. Constraints (3) ensure that each warehouse acts as a transshipment point: everything shipped into a warehouse is shipped out again. Constraints (4) ensure that for each customer, the total received minus the total passed on to the other customer meets its demand. Constraints (5) limit the flow on every arc to the common arc capacity, and constraints (6) ensure that all flows are non-negative.

Why the second sum in (2) and (4) matters

In the optimal plan, plant 3 makes only 100 t but sends out 280 t, because it receives 180 t from plant 1. Without the second sum in (2), plant 3 would appear to break its capacity. Likewise customer 6 receives 580 t and passes 180 t on to customer 7, which is why (4) uses net inflow.

Written out for plant 1: (X12 + X13 + X14 + X15 + X16 + X17) − (X21 + X31) ≤ 200

Integer flows come free

All values of \(Q_i\), \(R_i\) and \(U\) are whole numbers, so this network model always has an optimal solution with whole-number flows. No integer constraint is needed.

One SUMIF pattern does all the work

SUMIF(Origin,node,Flow) adds every flow leaving a node; SUMIF(Destination,node,Flow) adds every flow arriving. Listing arcs in one long column means the same two formulas build every flow balance constraint, however large the network gets.

Why does cost stop falling after 300 t?

At 300 t no arc limit binds any more: plant 2's 300 t can all travel on its cheapest routes. Extra arc capacity is worth nothing once the constraint is slack, so its shadow price is zero.

Your count of full arcs differs from the Excel table. Why?

At some capacities there are several plans with exactly the same minimum cost. Solver and this page can land on different ones, so the cost matches but the number of full arcs may not. Multiple optima are good news for a manager, who can pick whichever plan suits them.

    Why can no more than 20 t ever be lost?

    Plants can make 600 t and customers need 580 t. Anything lost in a warehouse has to be made up by extra production, and there is only 20 t of it. As the loss rate rises, the model sends less through warehouses so the total lost stays at or below 20 t, and pays for dearer direct routes instead.

    How would you change the Excel model?

    Add an input cell for the retention rate, then change each warehouse formula so that it compares retention × inflow with outflow, for example =0.9*SUMIF(Destination,H15,Flow)-SUMIF(Origin,H15,Flow), constrained to equal 0. The flows are then no longer guaranteed to be whole numbers.
