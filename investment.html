00

Problem type: multi-period financial models

A multi-period financial model plans how money moves through time. Investments take cash out in some years and pay cash back in later years, and any cash not invested earns a safe rate. The model decides how much to put into each investment so that the money is in the right place at the right time.

How to recognise one

  • There are several time periods, and money carries over from one to the next.
  • Each investment has a schedule: when you pay in and when you get paid back, per £1 or $1 invested.
  • You can only spend cash on hand, including returns from earlier investments.
  • Spare cash sits in a money market account earning a fixed rate.
  • The aim is usually the most cash at the end, or the least cash at the start to meet future payments.

The key modelling idea: a cash balance every year

Each year has one balance constraint, exactly like an inventory balance in aggregate planning:

\[\text{cash out}_t + \text{cash kept}_t = \text{cash kept}_{t-1}(1+r) + \text{cash in}_t\]

Check yourself: is it a multi-period financial model?

A university invests a donation in deposits of 1, 2 and 3 years so it can pay a scholarship every September.

Yes. Deposits pay out at known times, and each year's cash must cover that year's scholarship. It is a cash-flow matching model like the pension fund.

A company chooses which of seven projects to fund this year from a fixed budget.

Not quite. Projects are all or nothing and there is a single budget, so it is a capital budgeting (knapsack) model. With costs spread over several years it gains multi-period budget constraints.

An investor chooses stock weights to minimise risk for a target return.

No. There is one period and risk is measured by variance, which is quadratic. That is a portfolio selection model.

Growing $100,000 over four years at Barney-Jones

Barney-Jones has $100,000 to invest at the start of year 1. Five investments are available at different times. It can put at most $75,000 into any one investment, and uninvested cash earns 3% a year. What strategy gives the most cash at the start of year 5?

Starting cash$100,000start of year 1
Cap per investment$75,000for each of A to E
Money market3%a year on spare cash
Predict first: D returns 90% in one year. Why not put everything into D?

D can only be bought at the start of year 4, and the cap limits it to $75,000. Before then the money has to work somewhere else, and it has to come back as cash in time for year 4.

Model formulation

The model formulation uses the notation in the table. Constraints (2) and (3) are the yearly cash balances.

Objective function:

\[\max \; (1+r)M_4 + \sum_{i\in I} R_{i5} X_i\]
(1)
Example

Subject to:

\[\sum_{i\in I} O_{i1} X_i + M_1 = C_0\]
(2)

(3)
Example
\[X_i \le U \qquad \forall i \in I\]
(4)
Example

NotationDescription
Sets\(I\)Set of investments, indexed by \(i\), \(I = \{A,B,C,D,E\}\)
\(T\)Set of years, indexed by \(t\), \(T = \{1,\dots,5\}\) (cash moves at the start of each year)
Parameters\(C_0\)Cash available at the start of year 1, $100,000
\(U\)Most that can be put into any one investment, $75,000
\(r\)Money market interest rate, 3% a year
\(O_{it}\)Cash paid into investment \(i \in I\) at the start of year \(t \in T\), per $1 invested
\(R_{it}\)Cash returned by investment \(i \in I\) at the start of year \(t \in T\), per $1 invested
Decision variables\(X_i\)Dollars invested in investment \(i \in I\)
\(M_t\)Cash left in the money market after investing at the start of year \(t\), \(t = 1,\dots,4\)
Variables95 investments + 4 cash balances
Balances4years 1 to 4
Model typeLPmulti-period

The objective (1) maximises cash at the start of year 5: the money market balance after a year's interest plus the year 5 returns. Constraint (2) ensures that the starting cash is either invested or placed in the money market in year 1. Constraints (3) are the cash balances for years 2 to 4: what is invested plus what is kept equals last year's balance with interest plus this year's returns. Constraints (4) cap each investment at $75,000, and constraints (5) ensure that no investment or balance is negative, so Barney-Jones never spends cash it does not have.

Why does C drop out when the cap reaches $125,000?

With a higher cap, A alone can take all $100,000 in year 1 and its returns fund B, E and D. C's quick 20% is no longer needed to bridge to year 2, so the model drops it.

Why does the interest rate barely change the plan?

The money market only holds cash that cannot be invested because of the caps, so the rate changes final cash a little but not the investment decisions, until it becomes competitive with the investments themselves.
