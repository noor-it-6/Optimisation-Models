Problem type: transportation models

A transportation model ships one product from a set of origins directly to a set of destinations. It decides how many units go on each origin to destination route so that every destination gets its demand, no origin ships more than its capacity, and total cost is as low as possible.

How to recognise one

  • There are two groups of locations: origins with a limited supply and destinations with a demand.
  • Product goes directly from an origin to a destination. There are no warehouses or stops in between.
  • Each route has a cost per unit. It can include the production cost at the origin as well as the freight.
  • The decisions are how many units on each route: with m origins and n destinations there are m × n variables and m + n constraints.

The transportation tableau

The whole model fits in one table. Each row is an origin, each column a destination, and each cell a decision variable. Row totals must not exceed supply; column totals must meet demand.

Check yourself: is it a transportation model?

A department assigns four lecturers to four modules so total preparation time is lowest, one module each.

Yes, a special case. Each lecturer supplies 1 and each module demands 1, so it is an assignment model, which is a transportation model with all supplies and demands equal to 1.

RedBrand ships tomato product from plants, possibly through warehouses, to customers.

Not a pure transportation model. Product can stop at warehouses and pass between plants, so it is the more general logistics model.

A bakery chain sends bread from 2 bakeries to 6 shops each morning at least delivery cost.

Yes. Bakeries are origins with limited output, shops are destinations with orders, and every delivery is direct.

Shipping cars from 3 plants to 4 regions

Grand Prix Automobile makes cars at three plants and ships them to four regions of the country. Find the least-cost shipping plan that stays within plant capacities and meets every region's demand.

Plant capacity1,550450 + 600 + 500 cars
Regional demand1,250450 + 200 + 300 + 300 cars
Spare capacity300cars that will not be made
Routes12every plant to every region

Why ≤ for plants and ≥ for regions

Supply (1,550) is larger than demand (1,250), so plants cannot all ship their full capacity. Plant constraints are therefore "at most", while each region must receive "at least" its demand.

The objective (1) minimises the total cost of shipping cars from plants to regions. Constraints (2) ensure that for each plant, the total number of cars shipped to all regions does not exceed the plant's capacity. Constraints (3) ensure that for each region, the total number of cars received from all plants meets its demand. Constraints (4) ensure that all shipments are non-negative.

Row sums and column sums

Each plant constraint is a row sum of the plan and each region constraint is a column sum. That is why the tableau layout is so convenient for transportation problems. For larger networks, the list-of-arcs layout with SUMIF used in RedBrand scales better.

What if demand changes in every region?

The same percentage change is applied to all four regions, as in the SolverTable sheet. The model is re-solved as you drag.

Total cost176,050$
Total demand1,250of 1,550 capacity
Why does the model become infeasible above +20%?

At +25% total demand is 1.25 × 1,250 = 1,562.5 cars, more than the 1,550 the plants can make. No shipping plan can meet every region, so Solver reports "Not feasible".

Why does the line get steeper as demand rises?

Solver always uses the cheapest capacity first. Each extra car has to travel on a dearer route than the last, because the cheap routes and the plants serving them fill up. So cost rises faster and faster: about $1,900 per 1% at −20%, but about $2,660 per 1% above 0%, where Plants 1 and 3 are full and every extra car comes from Plant 2.

Why does the value change as capacity grows?

A shadow price holds only over a range (the allowable increase and decrease). Plant 1's −119 holds from 300 to 550 cars. Beyond that, the cheapest way to use the extra capacity changes, so the slope of the line changes too.

Plant 3 to Region 4 costs 180 and is unused. How much cheaper must it get before Solver uses it?

Its reduced cost in the Sensitivity Report is 13, so the unit cost must fall by more than $13, to below $167, before that route enters the optimal plan.
